Where monday.com wins, and where this does

These tools are not competing for the same job. Choosing the wrong one is not a pricing mistake, it is a category mistake, and it shows up about three weeks in.

monday.com wins on general work management. Running many teams' work in one place — intake, ownership, status, the operational routines a company builds around a board — is what it is built for, and Ingantt has none of that. There are no boards here for the marketing team's requests, the support queue, and the hiring pipeline. If what you are replacing is how your whole company tracks work, this is not the tool, and that is worth knowing on the first page rather than the fourth.

Ingantt wins when the plan has a shape. When work has to happen in an order, when one task cannot start until another finishes, when the completion date is a consequence of durations and dependencies rather than an aspiration in a column, and when someone senior is going to ask what the date depends on and expect a defensible answer. That is Critical Path Method, and it is either in the engine or it is not.

Many teams end up running both, and that is a perfectly sensible outcome rather than a failure to choose. The board stays where day-to-day work lives. The schedule — the construction programme, the release plan, the migration, the client delivery with a contractual date on it — lives somewhere that can calculate. What tips the decision is usually an external file: the moment a client or contractor sends a .MPP or an XER, a board cannot open it and a scheduling tool can.

Stay with monday.com

  • Day-to-day operational routines

monday.com and Ingantt — Questions People Actually Ask

Export the board as CSV or Excel and import the CSV. The rows come across — task names, dates, durations, owners — and you add the dependency network on top, which tends to be quick because you already know what waits on what. The caveat to know first is that a CSV carries rows, not a scheduling model, so dependencies, constraints, calendars, and baselines that were never in the file cannot be recovered from it. If the plan originally came out of Microsoft Project or Primavera P6, open that .MPP, XML, or XER file instead — it carries the whole model.

For project scheduling, yes. As a replacement for how a company tracks all of its work, no — and pretending otherwise would waste your time. monday.com is a general work-management platform; Ingantt is a scheduling tool with a Critical Path Method engine, resource leveling, baselines, and Earned Value. If the reason you are looking is that your timeline view cannot tell you what the finish date depends on, this is the swap. If it is that you want a cheaper board for the whole company, it is not.

Our comparison grades monday.com as not having real CPM scheduling, and we hold that grade openly rather than softening it. The distinction is between drawing a timeline and calculating one: a real Critical Path Method engine derives the finish date from the dependency network and durations, identifies which chain of tasks is driving it, and recalculates both when anything changes. That is the capability being compared, and it is the one that matters when a date slips.

Calculate. 4 dependency types with lag, 8 constraint types, custom calendars and work weeks, multiple critical paths, and rescheduling that cascades a change through the whole plan. Resource leveling by priority, slack, or ID with rate tables A–E and time-varying availability. Tracking with 11 baselines, 10 interim plans, and 55 baseline plus 5 variance columns. Earned Value with 13 columns including BCWS, BCWP, CPI, SPI, EAC, VAC, and TCPI. And 10 views on the same plan — including a Task Board, so the board view does not disappear.

So the people who want to see this week's work as cards can, while the schedule underneath remains a real network with a calculated critical path.
